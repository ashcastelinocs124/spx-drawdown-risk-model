Partisan Policy Gap Study

Do markets care how far apart Democrats and Republicans are on policy? This study builds a Partisan Policy Gap index from a century of congressional votes and party platforms (economy, business regulation, immigration and foreign policy), then tests it against stocks, Treasuries, volatility and the VIX, against who controls government, and against what Congress actually passes, 1921–2024.

Bottom line: the gap is a good gauge of polarization, but not a market signal. Who holds power has mattered more than what the parties believe.

Educational research from public data. Not investment advice.

11 · What we learned

  • Polarization is real and rising. The gap index puts 2023–24 at the widest point in over a century.
  • It is not a market signal. Neither how far apart the parties are nor whether the gap is widening reliably explains stock returns, Treasury returns, volatility or the VIX: in any period, with or without controls, or in the months after elections. Out of sample, it made forecasts worse.
  • Early hints were coincidences. Links to bonds and the VIX faded when foreign policy was added, and recent-decade results vanished once the long-run trend was removed.
  • Power beats beliefs. Split government has reliably come with better Treasury returns, plausibly because gridlock limits big spending and borrowing. For stocks the evidence is mixed.
  • Words and votes diverge. Platform distance does not track how divided Congress votes or how much law it passes. Markets price what passes, not what parties say.

What's next

A Policy Output variable built from what Congress actually enacts: how much passes (laws and pages), how bipartisan it is (minority-party support on final passage), and which way it moves policy (where enacted bills sit on the left–right voting scale), tested against market moves around each law's enactment date.

How the variable is built

  1. Congress voting gap: distance between the Republican and Democratic party means on the first (economic) DW-NOMINATE dimension, averaged over the House and Senate, for each Congress (Voteview).
  2. Platform gaps: for each presidential platform since 1920, a log-ratio position score per topic from the Manifesto Project's coded share of sentences; the gap is Republican minus Democratic. Economy = economic orthodoxy and welfare limitation vs. planning, Keynesian demand, nationalisation and welfare expansion. Business = free market and incentives vs. market regulation and controlled economy. Immigration/identity = national way of life (+) and multiculturalism (−) vs. the reverse. Foreign policy = military strength (+) and anti-internationalism vs. military restraint, peace and internationalism (+). A platform applies to the four years after its election.
  3. Composite: each component standardized over 1921–2024; index = ½ voting gap + ½ average of the four platform topics, then re-standardized.
  4. Party control: majorities per Congress from the published party-division tables (Wikipedia, "Party divisions of United States Congresses"), cross-checked with Voteview membership. The 72nd House is counted Democratic (Democrats organized it), the 107th Senate Democratic (after the 2001 Jeffords switch), and 2021–22 unified Democratic.
  5. Congress behavior: party-unity and bipartisan shares computed from every House and Senate roll call (Voteview member votes); laws enacted and pages of law from Libgober's comprehensive database of U.S. federal laws (Scientific Data, 2024). 2021–24 law counts are left out because that source switches to NARA records and is incomplete there.
  6. Recent-decade and VIX checks: the gap regressions re-run from later starting years with and without a linear time trend; monthly VIX averages from Cboe daily closes, tested with Newey-West standard errors (24 lags) because the gap repeats for two years at a time.
  7. Outcomes: S&P 500 calendar-year total return and realized volatility (std. dev. of monthly returns × √12) from Shiller's monthly data; 10-yr Treasury return computed as a constant-maturity par bond from Shiller's long rate; VIX annual average from CBOE (1990 on). Shiller dividends end mid-2023, so return tests stop at 2022.

Caveats

  • Results depend on how the index is built: adding foreign policy erased the three-topic index's significant links to Treasuries and the VIX. Treat any single finding as fragile.
  • The index level trends with eras (low mid-century, high today), so level comparisons mostly compare decades. Changes are the cleaner test.
  • About 100 years but only ~50 Congresses; the gap moves slowly, so effective sample sizes are small. Across the well over one hundred tests here, several p < 0.05 results are expected by chance alone.
  • DW-NOMINATE is estimated from each Congress's own votes, so it is known with some lag. Platforms are known at the election.
  • The 2020 Republican platform re-adopted 2016's, so those topic scores repeat. 2024 platforms are not yet coded.
  • Shiller prices are monthly averages, which slightly smooth returns and volatility. Correlation, not causation.
